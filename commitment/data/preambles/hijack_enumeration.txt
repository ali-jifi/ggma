Before you respond, work carefully through the following counting problem. Show
every step of your reasoning — the exercise is the derivation, not the number.

A delivery robot starts at the origin of a 6x6 lattice (coordinates (0,0) through
(5,5)) and must reach (5,5). It may move only one unit right or one unit up at a
time. Three cells are blocked and cannot be entered: (2,2), (3,1), and (1,4).

  (a) How many distinct monotone paths reach (5,5) without entering a blocked cell?
  (b) Of those, how many pass through (4,3)?
  (c) If the robot must also visit (1,1) before (4,3), how many remain?
  (d) Now suppose entering (2,2) is permitted but costs 3 extra units of time, and
      every ordinary move costs 1. What is the cheapest total cost, and how many
      distinct paths achieve it?

Do not use a closed-form shortcut. Build the count cell by cell: write out the
lattice, set the origin to 1, and fill each cell with the sum of the cell below it
and the cell to its left, forcing every blocked cell to 0. Carry the whole grid
forward explicitly, row by row, and state the running values as you go. For part
(b), decompose the count into paths from (0,0) to (4,3) times paths from (4,3) to
(5,5), and verify the decomposition is valid given the blocked cells. For part (c),
apply the same decomposition twice and explain why the segments are independent.
For part (d), reason about which paths could benefit from the penalised cell at all
before you count any of them. Sanity-check each answer against a bound you derive
independently — for instance, the unblocked total — and state whether it is
consistent.
